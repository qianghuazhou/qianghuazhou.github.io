Blood Products

Reference page for major transfusion products including red blood cells, platelets, plasma, cryoprecipitate, and granulocytes, with product-specific indications, compatibility considerations, practical dosing notes, and common cautions.

This page is intended as a practical transfusion medicine reference. Local institutional policies, product availability, and patient-specific factors should always be considered.

General Principles

Use the specific product needed

Transfuse the component that corrects the clinical problem rather than using blood products empirically. For example, use RBCs for oxygen-carrying support, platelets for thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction, plasma for multiple coagulation factor deficiencies, and cryoprecipitate for hypofibrinogenemia.

Transfusion is not benign

Risks include volume overload, hemolytic reactions, febrile and allergic reactions, TRALI, TACO, alloimmunization, infection risk, and metabolic complications.

Choose modified products when indicated

Depending on the clinical scenario, products may need leukoreduction, irradiation, washing, CMV-risk reduction, antigen matching, or HLA matching.

Common product modifications
  • Leukoreduced: reduces febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reactions, HLA alloimmunization, and CMV transmission risk.
  • Irradiated: prevents transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease by inactivating donor lymphocyte proliferation.
  • Washed: reduces plasma proteins; useful in selected cases such as recurrent severe allergic/anaphylactic reactions or IgA deficiency with anti-IgA.
  • Antigen-matched: often used in chronically transfused patients, especially sickle cell disease, to reduce alloimmunization.

Red Blood Cells (RBCs) Cellular Component

Packed red blood cells are used to increase oxygen-carrying capacity in anemia or blood loss. They are the most commonly transfused component.

Contents
  • Concentrated red cells suspended in additive solution
  • Minimal plasma compared with whole blood
  • Usually leukoreduced in Canada and many other jurisdictions
Main indications
  • Symptomatic anemia
  • Acute hemorrhage
  • Perioperative blood loss or anemia affecting tissue oxygen delivery
  • Chronic transfusion support, such as in sickle cell disease or marrow failure states
  • Exchange transfusion settings in selected disorders
Typical practice points
  • In adults, 1 unit usually increases hemoglobin by about 10 g/L.
  • In infants and small children, 10–15 mL/kg typically increases hemoglobin by about 20–30 g/L.
  • Restrictive transfusion strategies are generally preferred unless there is active bleeding, ischemia, or another specific indication.
  • RBC transfusion should not be used simply as a volume expander when oxygen-carrying support is not needed.
Important cautions
  • Requires ABO compatibility and appropriate pretransfusion testing.
  • Risk of hemolytic transfusion reaction if incorrect unit is administered.
  • May contribute to TACO, hyperkalemia, hypothermia, iron overload, and alloimmunization.
  • In chronic transfusion settings, monitor ferritin and iron burden.
Product-specific situations
  • Irradiated RBCs: for stem cell transplant recipients, selected immunocompromised patients, intrauterine transfusion, and other recognized indications.
  • Washed RBCs: for selected patients with severe allergic or anaphylactic reactions, especially when related to plasma proteins.
  • Phenotype- or genotype-matched RBCs: especially important in sickle cell disease and selected alloimmunized patients.
  • CMV-risk-reduced RBCs: commonly met by leukoreduced components in many settings.

Platelets Cellular Component

Platelet transfusion is used to prevent or treat bleeding caused by thrombocytopenia or clinically significant platelet dysfunction. Use should be guided by the bleeding risk, clinical context, procedure type, platelet count, and expected platelet recovery.

Platelet basics
  • Manufacturing: Platelets are produced from whole blood donations as pooled/buffy coat products or collected by single-donor apheresis.
  • Storage: Stored at room temperature with continuous agitation.
  • Shelf life: Commonly up to 7 days, although local expiry may vary by product type and pathogen-reduction practice.
  • Adult dose: Usually 1 apheresis platelet unit or 1 pooled platelet dose.
  • Administration: Typically infused over 30–60 minutes and completed within 4 hours.
  • Pathogen-reduced platelets: May be treated with psoralen/UV technology and stored in platelet additive solution (PAS), depending on local inventory.
Types of platelet products

Apheresis platelets

Single-donor platelet collection. May reduce donor exposure and can be selected for HLA compatibility when needed.

Pooled platelets

Whole blood–derived platelets pooled from multiple donations. In leukoreduced systems, they are generally considered clinically equivalent to apheresis platelets for routine use.

Modified platelets

May be pathogen-reduced, PAS-based, irradiated, washed, HLA-matched, HPA-selected, or crossmatch-compatible depending on indication and availability.

Hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia

Definition: Hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia is thrombocytopenia caused primarily by reduced bone marrow platelet production due to impaired megakaryopoiesis, marrow failure, marrow replacement, cytotoxic therapy, or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.

  • Common causes: chemotherapy, radiation therapy, acute leukemia, myelodysplastic neoplasms, aplastic anemia, marrow infiltration, and post-transplant marrow suppression.
  • Clinical relevance: thrombocytopenia may be severe and prolonged, so prophylactic platelet support is commonly required.
  • Key distinction: the main problem is decreased production rather than peripheral immune destruction or consumption.
Main indications
  • Prophylaxis in severe hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia
  • Treatment of active bleeding associated with thrombocytopenia
  • Support before invasive procedures or surgery
  • Selected cases of clinically significant qualitative platelet dysfunction
  • Massive hemorrhage protocols
Common transfusion thresholds
Platelet count threshold Typical clinical context
≤10 × 109/L Prophylaxis for stable, non-bleeding patients with hypoproliferative thrombocytopenia.
≤20 × 109/L Often used when bleeding risk is increased, such as fever, sepsis, mucosal bleeding, or additional clinical risk factors.
<20 × 109/L Central venous catheter placement in many practices, depending on site and local policy.
<20–50 × 109/L Lower-risk invasive procedures, depending on procedural site, operator, and patient bleeding risk.
<50 × 109/L Active bleeding, major non-neuraxial surgery, or significant invasive procedures.
>80 × 109/L Often targeted for epidural or neuraxial anesthesia, depending on local anesthesia policy.
<100 × 109/L Neurosurgery, central nervous system bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, or traumatic brain injury.
Selected therapeutic situations
  • Clinically significant bleeding: transfuse based on bleeding severity and clinical context; transfusion may be appropriate even above 10 × 109/L.
  • Antiplatelet drug-associated bleeding: may be considered in selected severe bleeding scenarios; effectiveness depends on the agent, timing of last dose, and clinical context.
  • Massive hemorrhage: platelet support is guided by local protocol, clinical bleeding, and serial laboratory assessment.
  • Qualitative platelet dysfunction: may be considered for clinically important bleeding or urgent intervention.
Compatibility and product selection
  • ABO compatibility: ABO-identical platelets are preferred when feasible because they generally provide better post-transfusion increments and may reduce refractoriness.
  • Minor ABO incompatibility: PAS or plasma-reduced platelets may reduce hemolytic risk in selected settings.
  • Rh compatibility: RhD-negative females of childbearing potential who receive RhD-positive platelets may require Rh immune globulin prophylaxis depending on product type and local policy.
  • Leukoreduction: standard in many jurisdictions; reduces febrile reactions, HLA alloimmunization, and CMV transmission risk.
  • Apheresis vs pooled platelets: when leukoreduced products are available, whole blood–derived platelets can generally be used as equivalent routine products to apheresis platelets.
Special platelet products
  • HLA-matched or crossmatch-compatible platelets: for immune platelet refractoriness, most often due to anti-HLA antibodies.
  • HPA-selected platelets: considered in selected patients with anti-HPA antibodies.
  • Washed platelets: for recurrent severe allergic reactions, IgA deficiency with anti-IgA when IgA-deficient donor platelets are unavailable, or selected neonatal settings.
  • Irradiated platelets: for patients at risk for transfusion-associated graft-versus-host disease.
  • Maternal washed platelets: may be used in neonatal alloimmune thrombocytopenia in selected situations.
Platelet refractoriness

Definition: Repeated poor platelet count increments after platelet transfusion.

  • Non-immune causes are more common: sepsis, fever, bleeding, DIC, splenomegaly, consumption, medications, and critical illness.
  • Immune causes: most often anti-HLA antibodies; less commonly anti-HPA antibodies.
  • Timing matters: poor 10-minute to 1-hour increment suggests immune-mediated destruction; poor later increment more often suggests non-immune consumption or sequestration.
  • Management: confirm refractoriness, assess non-immune causes, check post-transfusion increments, and use HLA-matched, HPA-selected, or crossmatch-compatible platelets when appropriate.
Situations where platelet transfusion may be ineffective or avoided
  • Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP): generally avoided unless serious bleeding or urgent procedure.
  • Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP): avoid unless life-threatening bleeding.
  • Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT): generally avoided unless major bleeding or urgent invasive need.
  • Post-transfusion purpura: specialist transfusion medicine guidance is required.
  • Uremic bleeding: usually better addressed with DDAVP, dialysis optimization, anemia correction, or other measures unless thrombocytopenia is also present.
Risks of platelet transfusion
  • Common: febrile non-hemolytic transfusion reactions and mild allergic reactions.
  • Serious but uncommon: bacterial contamination/sepsis, TRALI, TACO, hemolytic reactions, and severe allergic/anaphylactic reactions.
  • Long-term: HLA alloimmunization and platelet refractoriness, especially with repeated transfusions.
Alternatives and adjuncts
  • Tranexamic acid may be useful in selected mucosal or procedural bleeding settings.
  • DDAVP may help in selected qualitative platelet dysfunction states, especially uremic platelet dysfunction.
  • Topical or procedural hemostatic measures are useful for localized bleeding.
  • Adjuncts do not replace platelet transfusion when there is severe thrombocytopenia with active bleeding or urgent invasive need.
Practical note
  • Use restrictive prophylactic thresholds when clinically appropriate.
  • A poor post-transfusion increment should prompt assessment for bleeding, sepsis, splenomegaly, DIC, fever, consumption, drug effect, or immune refractoriness.
  • Do not reflexively request HLA-matched platelets until refractoriness is confirmed and non-immune causes are assessed.

Plasma (FFP / FP24 / Thawed Plasma) Plasma Component

Plasma provides multiple coagulation factors and is used when there is clinically relevant coagulation factor deficiency, usually affecting several factors at once.

Contents
  • All soluble coagulation factors
  • Albumin and other plasma proteins
  • Minimal or no viable cellular content
Main indications
  • Active bleeding with multiple coagulation factor deficiency
  • Massive transfusion
  • DIC with bleeding
  • Urgent warfarin reversal if PCC is unavailable or insufficient
  • Therapeutic plasma exchange in TTP
  • Liver disease with bleeding or prior to urgent invasive procedure in selected cases
What plasma is not for
  • Routine volume expansion
  • Nutritional protein replacement
  • Mild INR prolongation without bleeding or procedure
  • Correction of laboratory abnormalities alone without a clinical indication
Compatibility and dosing
  • AB plasma is the universal plasma donor.
  • Plasma should generally be ABO compatible with the recipient.
  • Typical dose is 10–15 mL/kg.
  • Small doses are often inadequate to meaningfully change coagulation factor levels.
Important cautions
  • May cause allergic reactions, TRALI, and TACO.
  • Large volumes may be required to correct coagulopathy, especially in adults.
  • Not a substitute for targeted products such as fibrinogen replacement when the major deficiency is low fibrinogen.

Cryoprecipitate Fibrinogen-Rich Component

Cryoprecipitate is prepared from plasma and contains concentrated fibrinogen and selected other proteins. Its main current role is fibrinogen replacement.

Contents
  • Fibrinogen
  • Factor VIII
  • von Willebrand factor
  • Factor XIII
  • Fibronectin
Main indications
  • Hypofibrinogenemia with bleeding
  • Massive hemorrhage with low fibrinogen
  • DIC with fibrinogen depletion
  • Inherited fibrinogen deficiency when fibrinogen concentrate is unavailable
Practical thresholds
  • Often considered when fibrinogen is <1.5–2.0 g/L in bleeding patients, depending on context.
  • In obstetric hemorrhage and major bleeding, higher fibrinogen targets may be used.
Important points
  • Cryoprecipitate is generally preferred over plasma when the principal issue is fibrinogen deficiency.
  • Volume is much lower than plasma for fibrinogen replacement.
  • ABO compatibility is often preferred when feasible, but is generally less critical than for plasma or RBCs.

Granulocytes Specialized Cellular Component

Granulocyte transfusions are uncommon and are reserved for selected patients with severe neutropenia and serious infection when standard therapy is insufficient and marrow recovery is expected.

Main indications
  • Profound neutropenia, often <0.5 × 109/L
  • Documented severe bacterial or fungal infection
  • Failure to respond adequately to antimicrobial therapy
  • Reasonable expectation of marrow recovery
Important characteristics
  • Short shelf life
  • Usually given soon after collection
  • Should be irradiated
  • ABO compatibility is preferred
  • Crossmatch may be required because of red cell contamination
Important cautions
  • May cause febrile reactions, alloimmunization, pulmonary reactions, and volume-related complications.
  • Use is highly specialized and often guided directly by transfusion medicine and the treating service.

Albumin Plasma-Derived Protein Product

Albumin is a human plasma-derived protein product used for selected clinical indications. It should not be used routinely as a first-line volume replacement fluid or solely to correct a low serum albumin concentration.

Major indications

1. Large-volume paracentesis

For cirrhosis with >5 L ascites removed, give 25% albumin 6–8 g/L removed to reduce paracentesis-induced circulatory dysfunction. Albumin is generally unnecessary when ≤5 L is removed. Routine replacement is not supported for malignant ascites.

2. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis

Give albumin in addition to antibiotics, particularly when renal dysfunction risk is increased. 25% albumin: 1.5 g/kg on day 1 and 1.0 g/kg on day 3. Use dry weight when clinically appropriate and consider fluid-overload risk.

3. HRS-AKI

Albumin is part of HRS-AKI management and should not be considered definitive monotherapy. When clinically appropriate, an initial albumin challenge of 1 g/kg/day (maximum 100 g/day) for up to 2 days may be used. With vasoconstrictor therapy, commonly 20–40 g/day, individualized to volume status, renal response and respiratory status.

4. Therapeutic plasma exchange

5% albumin is commonly used as replacement fluid for many therapeutic plasma exchange procedures. Plasma is used when plasma components must be replaced, including therapeutic plasma exchange for TTP.

5% vs 25% albumin

5% — Think VOLUME

Iso-oncotic. Primarily used when intravascular volume expansion is the intended effect and as replacement fluid in therapeutic plasma exchange. Crystalloid remains first-line for routine resuscitation.

25% — Think CONCENTRATED ONCOTIC THERAPY

Hyperoncotic. Commonly used for large-volume paracentesis, SBP and HRS-AKI. Select concentration according to the clinical indication and desired physiologic effect—not the serum albumin level.

Uses not routinely supported
  • Hypoalbuminemia: low serum albumin alone is not an indication. Do not infuse albumin simply to normalize a laboratory value.
  • Routine resuscitation: albumin has not shown a clear mortality advantage over crystalloid; crystalloids remain first-line for most patients.
  • Sepsis / septic shock: not routine; may be considered selectively after substantial crystalloid exposure.
  • Severe burns: crystalloid remains the basis of initial resuscitation; selected colloid use should generally be directed by a specialized burn service.
  • Intradialytic hypotension: insufficient evidence for routine albumin administration.
  • Cardiovascular surgery: routine use has not demonstrated superior clinical outcomes over crystalloid.
  • ARDS / acute lung injury: do not use routinely solely for ARDS or hypoalbuminemia.
Safety and monitoring
  • Monitor for fluid overload and pulmonary edema, increasing oxygen requirement, hypotension or hypertension, hemodilution, and infusion-related/allergic reactions.
  • Use particular caution in heart failure, pulmonary edema, renal impairment, significant hypervolemia, severe anemia, or other states where rapid intravascular expansion may be poorly tolerated.
  • Repeated concentrated albumin, particularly during HRS-AKI treatment, requires ongoing reassessment of volume and respiratory status.
Before ordering albumin
  1. Is there a defined indication? Do not order albumin simply because the serum albumin is low.
  2. Would crystalloid accomplish the same goal? For routine volume resuscitation, crystalloid is generally preferred.
  3. Which concentration fits the indication? 5% → volume; 25% → concentrated oncotic therapy / cirrhosis-related indications.
  4. What is the indication-specific dose? Dose to the indication, not a target serum albumin.
  5. Can the patient tolerate intravascular expansion? Reassess volume status, respiratory status, renal function and hemodynamics.
Bottom line: Albumin is an indication-specific therapy—not treatment for a laboratory value. First decide whether albumin is indicated, then select the concentration and dose according to the indication.
